The Electric Decade Reaches Half-Time

Ten years after the first mass-market lithium-ion vehicles crossed the one-million-unit threshold, the global EV market in 2025 looks nothing like the projections written in 2020. Growth outran forecasts, but so did the concentration of that growth in a handful of geographies and platforms.

Raghuram Sirigiri · Independent mobility research · Published Dec 2025
Reading time · 8 min
Abstract Global battery-electric passenger vehicle sales crossed 21 million units in 2025, a 7.4× rise since 2018. Regional adoption remains uneven: China alone accounts for over half of new EV registrations, and the top ten models together represent 34 % of global sales. Battery costs have fallen by more than half over the same window, opening a durable price gap over combustion equivalents in the compact segment. This retrospective traces the seven data patterns that most shaped the market and asks which ones are likely to hold through 2030.

01A market that grew faster than anyone modeled

The most striking fact about EVs at the end of 2025 is not that they are still growing — every analyst expected that — but how much faster the growth curve has been than the median forecast published five years ago. The IEA's 2020 Stated Policies scenario projected 14 million annual sales by 2030. That figure was cleared in 2024, six years early. Since then, both the optimistic and conservative scenarios have been revised upward twice.

The curve below shows global BEV unit sales for the past ten years alongside the same 2020 forecast for reference. The gap between the two widens non-linearly, driven mostly by the compact SUV segment in China.

What makes the miss so large is that the forecast wasn't especially conservative — it assumed the announced 2020 policy set would hold. Instead, Europe and China both accelerated their timelines, and unit economics improved fast enough that the sub-$30k price point cleared two years earlier than expected.

02Where the growth actually landed

Aggregate curves flatter the story. The regional breakdown makes clear that the last five years of expansion has been overwhelmingly a Chinese phenomenon — followed by Europe, with the rest of the world combined still below Europe's share.

The single most consequential number in the EV transition is not the price of lithium, or the range of the average pack — it is the fraction of Chinese new car sales that are already electric. — Wolfgang Schmidt, ICCT, panel remark at Munich Auto Week 2025

The concentration matters because it shapes the supply chain that everyone else will inherit. Battery cell manufacturing, cathode chemistry choices, and the standard fast-charging protocols are all being decided in a market that is largely closed to non-domestic OEMs.

03Ten models, one third of the market

Concentration shows up again in the model mix. The top ten best-selling EVs of 2025 together account for roughly 34 % of global unit sales — a level of concentration higher than in any comparable ICE year on record. Six of the ten are made in China, three in the United States, and one in Germany.

04Two chemistries, one strategic split

Under the hood, the industry has quietly bifurcated. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) has taken over the low- and mid-range of the market on the back of cost and safety, while nickel-based chemistries (NMC and NCA) continue to dominate premium and long-range packs. The split is now surprisingly clean — roughly 62 % LFP, 33 % NMC/NCA, and the balance is everything else, including early solid-state prototypes.

The scatter on the right hints at what the pricing frontier now looks like. Roughly $150 per additional mile of EPA range buys you a place on the trend line. Models above it are effectively paying a brand premium; those below tend to compete on utility or platform novelty.

05Charging finally caught up — mostly

Infrastructure has historically lagged demand, and 2020–2022 was a period where public fast-charger deployment fell noticeably behind vehicle additions. That gap has closed in most regions. China deployed roughly twice the DC fast-charge capacity as the rest of the world combined in 2025; Europe grew more slowly but from a healthier per-vehicle base.

06The cost curve that made all of this possible

None of the above would have been feasible without the collapse in battery cell cost. From roughly $180 per kWh in 2018, average cell prices fell below $75 per kWh in 2025 — with LFP cells trading at $58 for the largest procurement contracts. Over the same window, average pack energy density climbed from 220 to 293 Wh/kg. Those two curves, plotted together, are the closest thing this industry has to a Moore's Law.

07What to watch through 2030

Three questions will matter most for the second half of the decade. First, whether the LFP–NMC split converges as solid-state chemistries reach volume; second, whether North American EV share recovers or continues to compress; and third, whether any single OEM outside China can post a year with more than two million BEV deliveries. On present trajectory, the answer to the third is unlikely before 2027 — but current forecasts have been badly wrong before.

The safest prediction is the one this industry has spent a decade validating: whatever number appears in a 2025 baseline forecast for 2030, the realized figure will be higher.
